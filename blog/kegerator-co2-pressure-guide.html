Kegerator CO2 Pressure: How to Set It for Each Beer Style

By Vitalii Nestorenko · November 12, 2026 · 5 min read

There is no single correct CO2 pressure for a kegerator. The right setting depends on three things: how carbonated the beer is supposed to be, how cold the keg is, and where you live. Set the regulator to match those, and the beer pours clear with a proper head. Guess, and you get foam, flat pints, or a beer that slowly changes over a few weeks.

I brew beer professionally and install home bars in Calgary, so this question comes up constantly, especially now, when kegerators are a popular gift for the holidays. Here is how I think about it.

Start with volumes of CO2, not PSI

Brewers describe carbonation in "volumes of CO2". A beer with 2.5 volumes holds about two and a half times its own volume in dissolved gas. Each style has a typical range, and the pressure you set is only a means of holding the beer at that level.

Commonly published ranges, such as the chart at Brewer's Friend, look like this:

  • British-style ales: roughly 1.5 to 2.0 volumes, gently carbonated.
  • Porters and stouts: roughly 1.7 to 2.3 volumes.
  • American ales and lagers: roughly 2.2 to 2.7 volumes.
  • Belgian ales and saisons: higher, around 2.7 to 3.5 volumes.
  • German wheat beers: the highest, around 3.3 to 4.5 volumes.

These are guides, not laws. A brewery may carbonate a particular beer a little differently, so if you buy kegs from a local brewery, ask what the beer is carbonated to. That is the number to aim for.

Temperature changes the pressure you need

Cold liquid holds more dissolved CO2 than warm liquid. That means a colder keg reaches a given carbonation at a lower regulator pressure, and a warmer keg needs more. Pressure, temperature and volumes are linked, which is why published carbonation charts have temperature down one side and pressure across the other.

The practical lesson is to measure the temperature of the beer, not the room. A thermometer in a glass of water on the kegerator shelf tells you far more than the dial on the front. Once the temperature is steady, you can read the right pressure for your target off a chart or a calculator.

As a rough reference, one equipment maker suggests a kegerator at about 2 °C with the regulator near 12 psi will suit almost all styles, and another supplier notes that typical ales at 3 to 4 °C land around 10 to 12 psi. Treat those as starting points, then adjust to your beer.

Calgary's altitude matters a little

Calgary sits at roughly 1,050 metres above sea level. Draught equipment suppliers point out that elevation affects the pressure you need, and many published pressure charts are written for sea-level conditions. In practice this means that the chart number may need a small adjustment here, and the right fix is to confirm with the brewery or your supplier and then judge by how the beer actually pours and tastes.

I do not rely on a one-size rule of thumb. I set the pressure, pour a few glasses, and adjust in small steps.

Serving pressure versus carbonating pressure

Most beer you buy in a keg is already carbonated. In that case your regulator's job is to hold that level and push the beer to the tap, not to carbonate it. If you set the pressure too high, the beer slowly absorbs extra CO2 and becomes overcarbonated. Too low, and it slowly loses CO2 and goes flat, or foams because gas breaks out of the beer in the line.

Home brewers who "force carbonate" use higher pressure for a set time, then drop to serving pressure. If that is you, remember that the serving pressure must also be balanced against the beer line, which brings us to the next point.

Pressure and line length work together

The beer line creates resistance. A longer or narrower line slows the beer, a shorter or wider one lets it rush. If the line is too short for your pressure, the beer shoots out and foams even when the carbonation is right. This is why "just turn the pressure down" often creates a different problem: the beer is now under-pressured for its carbonation and foams in the line instead.

If pours are wild, check the line length and temperature before spending money on parts. I cover these in more detail in my kegerator installation guide.

Troubleshooting checklist

  1. Check the beer temperature with a real thermometer. Warm beer foams.
  2. Look up the target carbonation for the style you are serving.
  3. Match the regulator to that temperature and target, then wait. A keg can take a day or more to settle at a new pressure.
  4. Check line length and the tower. A warm tower gives a foamy first pour.
  5. Check for gas leaks. Spray soapy water on the fittings. If the cylinder empties faster than expected, find the leak.
  6. Clean the lines. Dirty lines cause foam and off flavours.

A safety note on CO2

CO2 displaces oxygen. A leaking cylinder in a small closed room is a genuine hazard. Always secure the cylinder upright so it cannot fall, and use gas-rated fittings. If you smell nothing yet the cylinder drains quickly, treat it as a leak and ventilate the space.

FAQ

What PSI should my kegerator be set to?

It depends on the beer's target carbonation and its temperature. Many ales at 3 to 4 °C sit around 10 to 12 psi, but check a carbonation chart or ask the brewery for the specific beer.

Can I use the same pressure for every beer?

For a mixed home setup, a moderate pressure works for many ales and lagers. Very high-carbonation styles such as wheat beers, and low-carbonation styles such as British ales, do better with their own setting.

Why is my beer foamy even at the right pressure?

Usually the temperature is too warm or uneven, the line is too short for the pressure, or the lines are dirty. Fix those before changing the regulator again.
