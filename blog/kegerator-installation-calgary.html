Kegerator Installation in Calgary: What a Brewer Checks First

By Vitalii Nestorenko · November 3, 2026 · 5 min read

A kegerator looks like a fridge with a tap on top, so it is easy to assume installation means plugging it in. In practice, most problems with home draught systems come from how the unit was fitted, balanced and maintained, not from the kegerator itself. Foamy pours, flat beer, a fridge that runs non-stop inside a cabinet, or a leak under the counter are all installation issues.

I brew beer professionally and also run a handyman business in Calgary, so I see both sides: what the beer needs, and what the house needs. Here is what I check before a kegerator goes in.

1. Where the kegerator will live

The location decides almost everything else. Before buying, measure the space and check three things.

  • Freestanding or built-in. A freestanding kegerator needs open space around it to shed heat. Only a model rated for built-in (front-venting) installation should be enclosed in cabinetry.
  • Floor and access. A full keg is heavy. The floor should be level and solid, and you need a clear path to roll kegs in and out without scraping drywall or flooring.
  • Power. A nearby dedicated outlet is ideal. Extension cords behind a cabinet are a bad idea for an appliance that runs for years.

In Calgary basements, I also look at temperature swings. An unheated corner near an exterior wall can get cold in January and warm in July, and the kegerator has to work harder to hold a steady temperature.

2. Ventilation: the most common mistake

The cooling system pushes heat out somewhere. If that heat has nowhere to go, the compressor runs constantly, the beer warms up, and the unit wears out early. This is the number one problem I see with DIY built-in setups: a freestanding kegerator slid into a tight cabinet opening.

If you want the clean, built-in look, either choose a front-venting model designed for it or modify the cabinetry to give the unit the clearance its manual specifies. Proper cabinet modification, with clean cuts, finished edges and vent openings, is a big part of what makes a home bar look professional instead of improvised.

3. The draught tower and counter

If the tap tower goes through a countertop or the top of a cabinet, the hole has to be the right size, in the right spot, and sealed. The tower needs to be mounted solidly so pulling the tap handle every day does not loosen it. Cold beer lines inside the tower also attract condensation, so the tower should be insulated (and, on longer runs, cooled) to avoid foamy first pours and water dripping into the cabinet.

4. CO2 setup and safety

Carbon dioxide is what pushes beer from the keg to the glass. Getting it right matters for the beer and for safety.

  • The cylinder should be secured upright so it cannot tip over.
  • The regulator and all connections should be checked for leaks with soapy water, not by ear.
  • CO2 displaces oxygen, so a leaking cylinder in a small closed space is a real hazard. Keep the system where a leak would be noticed, and never ignore a cylinder that empties much faster than expected.

Pressure has to match the beer. Different styles are carbonated to different levels, and the pressure that keeps a stout right will overcarbonate some lagers over time. Your brewery or keg supplier can tell you the target for the beer you buy, and the regulator should be set to match it.

5. Balancing the system so it pours properly

Foam is the classic home kegerator complaint. In most cases the cause is one of these:

  1. Temperature is not steady. Warm beer releases CO2 and foams. The industry's draught manual stresses holding a constant cold temperature all the way from keg to glass, including the line inside the tower.
  2. Pressure is too high or too low for the beer and temperature.
  3. The beer line is too short for the pressure. Line length and diameter create resistance that slows the beer down. Short lines on a high-pressure system make the beer shoot out and foam.
  4. Dirty lines, which also hurt flavour (more on that below).

A balanced system pours a clear pint with a neat head at a calm speed. If you have to fight the tap every time, something above is off, and it is usually fixable without buying new equipment.

6. Line cleaning: the part everyone forgets

Beer lines build up yeast, bacteria and mineral deposits. You will not see it, but you will taste it: buttery, sour or "stale bar" flavours that have nothing to do with the beer you bought. The Brewers Association recommends cleaning draught lines with a proper beer line cleaner about every two weeks, plus a periodic acid clean to remove mineral scale.

At home, a practical rule is to clean the lines every time you change a keg, and never let more than a few weeks pass. Calgary's hard water is another reason not to skip the acid clean. If you would rather not deal with it, line cleaning is something I offer as a regular service for clients with home draught systems.

7. Making it part of the room

A kegerator is usually one piece of a bigger plan: a basement bar, a games room, or an entertainment wall. That is where general handyman work comes in: mounting the TV on real studs, adding floating shelves for glassware, LED lighting, and hiding cables so the space looks finished. Planning it all together avoids drilling twice or discovering the outlet is behind the fridge.

DIY or professional install?

A freestanding kegerator on a basement floor is a reasonable DIY project if you follow the manual and leak-test the gas. It makes sense to bring in help when the unit is going into cabinetry, the tower goes through a countertop, you are building a full bar, or the system keeps pouring foam and you cannot find why. If any electrical work beyond plugging into an existing outlet is needed, that is a job for a licensed electrician.

FAQ

Can I put any kegerator inside a cabinet?

No. Only models designed for built-in use (usually front-venting) should be enclosed. A freestanding unit in a tight cabinet overheats, runs constantly and wears out early.

Why is my kegerator pouring mostly foam?

The usual causes are unstable temperature, wrong CO2 pressure for the beer, beer lines that are too short for the pressure, or dirty lines. Check them in that order before buying new parts.

How often should home beer lines be cleaned?

The Brewers Association recommends a line cleaning about every two weeks for draught systems. At home, cleaning at every keg change is a good habit.
