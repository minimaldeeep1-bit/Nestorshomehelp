Why Kegerator Beer Pours Foamy: A Brewer's Fix List

By Vitalii Nestorenko · November 5, 2026 · 5 min read

Foamy beer from a kegerator is almost never a "bad keg". It is nearly always one of five things: beer that is too warm, pressure that does not suit the beer, a line that does not push back enough, dirty lines, or a warm tower. Check them in that order and you will usually find the culprit in under an hour.

I brew beer professionally (Certified Cicerone, 8+ years), and I also install and fix home bars around Calgary, Airdrie, Chestermere, Okotoks and Cochrane. Here is the order I follow when a home draught system foams.

First, understand what foam actually is

Beer in the keg holds dissolved CO2. As long as the pressure on the beer matches the amount of gas dissolved in it, the gas stays put. When beer is forced through the line too fast, or warms up, or hits a rough surface, that gas comes out of solution as bubbles. That is foam.

So every fix below does one of two things: keeps the gas dissolved until the beer reaches the glass, or removes whatever makes the gas escape early.

Step 1: Check the beer temperature

Temperature is the first factor in any draught system, because it decides how much pressure the dissolved CO2 creates. Draught guidance commonly puts storage at roughly 34–38°F (about 1–3°C), and the higher the beer climbs above that, the more the pressure inside the keg rises and the more foam you get.

Do not trust the dial on the kegerator. Do this instead:

  1. Leave the tap untouched for a few hours.
  2. Pour the first glass and measure the beer itself with a thermometer.
  3. Compare it with the fridge setting.

If the first pour is clearly warmer than the fridge, beer is warming in the tower or the line between pours. That points to a tower or insulation problem (Step 5), not a pressure problem.

Step 2: Check the pressure against the beer

The right keg pressure depends on how carbonated the beer is and how cold it is. A highly carbonated wheat beer needs more pressure than a low-carbonation English-style ale at the same temperature. Setting one pressure for everything is a very common cause of trouble.

Two practical points:

  • Too much pressure pushes beer through the line too fast and also over-carbonates the beer over time.
  • Too little pressure lets gas break out of the beer inside the line, and you get foam plus, eventually, flat beer.

Make one adjustment at a time and give the system a day to settle. Changing pressure, line and temperature all at once means you will never know which one fixed it.

Step 3: Check line balance (the one people skip)

A balanced system is one where the pressure pushing the beer is offset by the resistance of the beer line. The resistance depends on line length, line diameter and the height difference between keg and faucet. If the line is too short or too wide, the beer rushes out and foams at the faucet.

This is the classic problem with kegerators sold with short, wide hose, or after someone shortens the line to "tidy up" a cabinet install. Home forum advice commonly points to narrow-bore (3/16 inch) line of several feet for typical home systems, but the exact length depends on your hose, your beer and your pressure, so treat any number you read online as a starting point and not a rule.

If you plan a built-in bar where the keg sits far from the tap, or the tap sits well above the keg, the line must be planned for that from the start. This is where an engineering approach helps: the layout is decided before anything is cut. See the home bar and kegerator installation service for how I plan these builds.

Step 4: Clean the lines

Dirty lines make gas break out of solution because beer film and yeast give bubbles somewhere to start. They also cause off flavours, so a line that foams is often a line that tastes wrong.

The Brewers Association's guidance, repeated by many draught suppliers, is a cleaning with an alkaline line cleaner at least every two weeks for commercial systems, with the faucet taken apart as well. Home systems that pour less often are less demanding, but if a keg takes a month or more to finish, clean the lines between kegs at least, and use a brush on the faucet and coupler.

A basic cleaning check:

  • Disassemble the faucet and soak the parts in cleaner.
  • Circulate cleaner through the line for the time the product label states (the BA mentions at least 15 minutes for commercial cleaning).
  • Rinse with plenty of clean water until there is no cleaner taste or smell.
  • Replace old, scratched or kinked line instead of trying to save it.

Step 5: Check the tower, shank and fittings

If the first pour is warm and foamy, then clears up after a glass or two, the beer is sitting in a warm part of the system. The tower and shank pass through the countertop, and without cooling or insulation they sit at room temperature while the keg is cold.

Look for:

  • An uninsulated or poorly sealed tower, especially on a built-in bar.
  • A loose shank or washer that lets air in or beer seep out.
  • A kinked or pinched line behind the cabinet.
  • Sunlight or a heat source (a furnace duct, a window) warming the tower.

Calgary's dry winter air and big temperature swings do not change the physics, but an unheated basement corner that is cold in January and warm in July can make a kegerator work harder and drift. Read more on placement in the kegerator installation guide.

Quick reference checklist

  1. Measure the temperature of the first pour. Is it close to the fridge setting?
  2. Is the pressure suited to this beer's carbonation?
  3. Is the line narrow and long enough to balance that pressure?
  4. When were the lines and faucet last cleaned?
  5. Is the tower insulated, sealed and away from heat?

FAQ

Why is only the first pour foamy?

The beer in the tower and faucet has warmed up between pours. Better insulation or a cooled tower fixes it. Pouring off a small amount first is a short-term workaround.

Can I fix foam by lowering the pressure?

Sometimes, but not always. If the line is too short or the beer is warm, lowering pressure can turn foam into flat, under-carbonated beer. Check temperature and line balance first.

How often should I clean my kegerator lines?

The Brewers Association recommends at least every two weeks for commercial systems. At home, clean between kegs at minimum, and sooner if the beer tastes off or starts to foam.
