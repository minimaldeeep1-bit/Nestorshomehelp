Best Landlord Maintenance Checklist for Rentals

By Vitalii Nestorenko · July 30, 2026 · 7 min read

A small drip under a bathroom vanity can become a damaged cabinet, mould concern, insurance claim, and unhappy tenant before the next rent payment is due. The best landlord maintenance checklist is not a stack of tasks to complete once a year. It is a working system for spotting small problems early, documenting the condition of your unit, and making repairs before they interrupt someone’s home.

For landlords, dependable maintenance protects more than the property. It protects tenant relationships, reduces vacancy risk, and makes every turnover less stressful. Calgary’s dry winters, sudden temperature changes, snow, and spring thaw can be especially hard on rental homes, condos, and exterior features. A clear routine gives you a practical way to stay ahead.

Start With Clear Responsibilities

A checklist works best when everyone knows what they are responsible for. Landlords are generally responsible for providing and maintaining a safe, habitable rental property. Tenants should report damage, leaks, pests, malfunctioning heat, and other concerns promptly, while also handling ordinary housekeeping and reasonable day-to-day care.

Put reporting expectations in writing. Ask tenants to notify you right away about water leaks, a toilet that will not stop running, electrical issues, loose handrails, damaged locks, or signs of pests. Make it easy for them to do that with one phone number or email address. A tenant who thinks a minor issue can wait until month-end may not realize how quickly water or cold air can cause damage.

It also helps to explain what counts as urgent. No heat during a Calgary cold snap, an active water leak, a sewer backup, a broken exterior lock, or a sparking outlet needs fast action. A scuffed wall or loose cabinet handle can normally be scheduled, but it should still be recorded and addressed.

Best Landlord Maintenance Checklist by Timing

The right schedule depends on the age and condition of the property. A newer condo with building-managed systems needs a different approach than an older detached home. Still, the following schedule covers the maintenance items that most often prevent expensive surprises.

Monthly or Between Tenant Reports

You do not need to enter a rental unit every month without a reason. Instead, use monthly reminders, maintenance requests, and permitted inspections to keep communication open. Ask tenants to test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms according to the device instructions and report any failed alarms right away.

Track recurring issues. If the same sink clogs twice, the fix may be more than clearing a blockage. If condensation keeps appearing around one window, look at seals, ventilation, and humidity rather than repainting the drywall each time.

Keep a simple maintenance record with the date reported, photos, work completed, invoices, and any follow-up needed. This is useful for budgeting, turnover planning, and showing that you addressed concerns responsibly.

Every Three to Six Months

Plan a permitted property inspection with proper notice under the tenancy agreement and applicable Alberta requirements. The goal is not to scrutinize a tenant’s personal belongings. It is to inspect the systems and surfaces that need maintenance.

During the visit, check the following:

  • Run every faucet and look under sinks for drips, swollen cabinetry, loose shutoff valves, or slow drains.
  • Flush toilets and check for constant running, loose seats, leaking supply lines, or poor flushing.
  • Test doors, windows, locks, weatherstripping, screens, and handles.
  • Check caulking around tubs, showers, backsplashes, and sinks for gaps, cracking, or mould staining.
  • Look for wall cracks, soft flooring, water marks, peeling paint, damaged blinds, and loose handrails.
  • Confirm bathroom and kitchen fans operate and vent properly where applicable.

Take dated photos of maintenance concerns, even if you are not repairing them that day. A clear record helps distinguish ordinary wear from new damage and gives you a reliable baseline at the next inspection.

Spring: Inspect for Thaw and Water Damage

Spring is when winter problems become visible. Snowmelt and freeze-thaw cycles can expose drainage, roofing, window, and foundation issues that were hidden for months.

Walk the exterior after the snow has melted. Check that downspouts direct water away from the foundation, eavestroughs are clear, and grading does not send water toward the home. Look for loose fence boards, damaged gates, lifted deck boards, and cracked concrete that could create a trip hazard.

Inside, inspect basements, utility rooms, windows, and exterior walls for dampness, staining, musty smells, or fresh cracks. If a unit has a sump pump, confirm it is operating as intended. Spring is also a good time to repair damaged screens, tighten loose door hardware, touch up paint, and refresh failed caulking before moisture gets behind finished surfaces.

Summer: Maintain Exterior and Ventilation

Summer is practical repair season. Dry conditions make it easier to handle exterior painting, fence repairs, deck maintenance, window sealing, and yard work. Address these early rather than waiting until rain or cold limits the work.

Inspect decks and stairs for loose fasteners, deteriorated wood, unstable railings, and peeling finishes. Check fences after high winds, especially around gates and posts. Trim vegetation away from siding, exterior vents, and air-conditioning equipment so moisture and debris do not build up.

For occupied rentals, make sure bathroom fans and range hoods are working. Good ventilation is not just about comfort. It helps reduce condensation, odours, and moisture damage in kitchens and bathrooms.

Fall: Prepare the Property for Winter

Fall is the highest-value season on a landlord maintenance checklist in Calgary. A few preventative tasks can avoid frozen pipes, heat complaints, water intrusion, and emergency calls when temperatures drop.

Service or inspect the heating system according to manufacturer recommendations. Replace furnace filters where the property’s setup requires it, and make sure tenants know how often filters should be changed. Test thermostats, confirm heat reaches all rooms, and address cold spots before winter.

Outside, clean eavestroughs, secure downspouts, inspect roof edges from the ground, and check weatherstripping around exterior doors and windows. Disconnect and drain exterior hoses where appropriate, shut off exterior water lines if the plumbing setup allows, and protect vulnerable taps from freezing.

Remind tenants to maintain reasonable indoor heat and report drafts, frozen pipes, or unusual furnace noises immediately. A vacant unit needs special attention. Confirm heat is on, arrange regular checks, and make sure water lines are protected according to your insurer’s requirements.

Winter: Respond Quickly and Monitor High-Risk Areas

Winter maintenance is largely about response time. Keep pathways, entrances, stairs, and handrails safe. Snow and ice removal responsibilities should be clear in the lease, but landlords should still verify that the property is reasonably safe and accessible.

Watch for ice dam warning signs, attic condensation, frozen plumbing, and heavy snow buildup where it could affect structures. If a pipe freezes, avoid guessing with open flames or improvised heating methods. Shut off water if there is a risk of a burst line and arrange qualified help promptly.

Indoor repairs matter in winter too. Loose doors, failed weatherstripping, cracked caulking, and damaged window locks can cause drafts and tenant discomfort. These are often straightforward repairs, but delaying them can raise heating costs and create bigger moisture issues.

Do Not Forget Turnover Maintenance

The move-out inspection is the best time to reset the rental and catch items that are easy to miss when a home is furnished. Compare the unit with the move-in inspection report and photos, then separate normal wear from damage needing repair.

Test every light switch, outlet, appliance, faucet, toilet, fan, lock, closet door, and smoke or carbon monoxide alarm. Check floors closely for water damage near dishwashers, refrigerators, toilets, and tubs. Repair drywall holes, loose trim, damaged caulking, sticking doors, and worn hardware before the next tenant arrives.

Turnover is also the right time to repaint touch-up areas, deep clean, replace burnt-out bulbs, and deal with small repairs in one coordinated visit. Booking several minor jobs together is often more efficient than calling different trades for each task.

Prioritize Safety Over Cosmetic Repairs

Not every maintenance issue carries the same risk. A chipped baseboard can wait. A loose stair railing, leaking toilet supply line, faulty lock, exposed wiring, or non-functioning smoke alarm should move to the top of the list.

When planning repairs, use three questions: Could this hurt someone? Could this cause fast property damage? Could this make the unit unlivable? If the answer is yes to any of them, act quickly and document what was done.

For electrical, plumbing, heating, structural, or code-sensitive work, use the right qualified professional. A handyman can be an efficient choice for many practical repairs, adjustments, installations, patching, caulking, painting, and exterior maintenance tasks. But the best decision depends on the scope of the issue and whether permits or a licensed trade are required.

Keep the Checklist Useful, Not Perfect

A maintenance plan only works if you can follow it. Start with seasonal inspections, a clear emergency process, and a record of every repair. Add property-specific reminders as you learn where the unit tends to need attention, whether that is a drafty patio door, aging caulking, a temperamental toilet, or a fence exposed to wind.

The best rental properties are rarely the ones with no maintenance needs. They are the ones where small concerns are reported early, handled properly, and never given the chance to become a much larger problem.
