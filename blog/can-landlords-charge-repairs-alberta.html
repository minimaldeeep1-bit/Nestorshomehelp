Can Landlords Charge Repairs? Alberta Rules

By Vitalii Nestorenko · October 12, 2026 · 6 min read

A dripping faucet, a hole in drywall, or a broken closet door can quickly turn into an awkward question between a tenant and landlord: can landlords charge repairs? In Alberta, the answer depends on why the repair is needed, how the damage happened, and whether the landlord follows the proper process.

For Calgary-area rental properties, the fairest approach is simple: landlords maintain the home; tenants pay for damage they, their guests, or their pets cause beyond ordinary use. The details matter, though. A rushed repair bill or a deduction from a security deposit without documentation can create a dispute that costs more time than the repair itself.

Can Landlords Charge Repairs in Alberta?

Landlords can charge a tenant for repairs when the tenant is responsible for damage. This may include damage caused by carelessness, misuse, neglect, or an accident involving the tenant, a household member, a guest, or a pet.

For example, a tenant may be responsible for repairing a punched hole in drywall, a cracked interior door caused by force, a clogged toilet caused by inappropriate items being flushed, or substantial pet damage to flooring. If a tenant leaves without reporting a leak and the delay causes further water damage, that may also affect responsibility.

However, a landlord cannot simply pass every repair cost to the tenant. The landlord remains responsible for keeping the rental property safe, habitable, and in good repair. Age, regular use, weather, and ordinary deterioration are part of owning a rental home.

The key question is not just, “What broke?” It is, “Why did it break?”

Normal Wear and Tear Is Not Tenant Damage

Normal wear and tear is the gradual decline that happens when someone lives in a home carefully and reasonably. It is expected, even with a good tenant, and it is usually the landlord’s cost.

Common examples include faded paint, worn carpet in high-traffic areas, loose cabinet hinges from years of use, minor scuffs, aging caulking, or an appliance that fails because it has reached the end of its useful life. A toilet fill valve that wears out or a faucet cartridge that begins to drip is generally a maintenance issue, not something a tenant should be charged for.

Tenant damage goes beyond that normal aging process. A large stain or burn in carpet, missing smoke detectors, broken blinds from rough handling, or deep gouges in flooring may be chargeable if the tenant caused the issue.

There can be grey areas. A small wall mark may be ordinary use, while many large holes from improperly mounted shelves or a TV may be considered damage. A repair professional can document what needs to be fixed, but the landlord and tenant still need to consider the cause, the property’s condition at move-in, and the reasonable cost of the work.

Repairs Landlords Usually Need to Cover

Landlords should plan and budget for ongoing maintenance. Passing routine ownership costs to a tenant is rarely reasonable and can lead to avoidable conflict.

A landlord will usually be responsible for issues such as plumbing leaks caused by worn parts, furnace or hot-water problems, electrical faults, appliance breakdowns due to age, roof leaks, deteriorating seals, and door or window issues caused by settlement or normal use. Safety concerns need prompt attention, especially issues involving water, electrical components, locks, heating, smoke alarms, or trip hazards.

A tenant should still report problems quickly. Reporting a slow leak early can mean a simple faucet repair. Ignoring it for weeks may allow water to damage cabinetry, flooring, or drywall. Fast communication protects both parties and helps keep the repair bill reasonable.

What Can a Landlord Charge for?

When a tenant is responsible, a landlord can seek a reasonable amount for the actual loss. “Reasonable” is the word that matters most. A tenant should not be billed for an unnecessary upgrade, a full replacement when a repair would do, or the brand-new value of an item that was already old and worn.

If a five-year-old carpet has a damaged section, the charge should account for its condition and remaining useful life. If a damaged door can be repaired, charging for a premium new door and full installation may not be justified. On the other hand, a repair that requires skilled labour, matching materials, disposal, and multiple visits can cost more than it first appears.

Clear invoices help. A proper repair record should show what was damaged, the work completed, labour and material costs, and before-and-after photos where practical. This protects a landlord against claims of overcharging and gives a tenant a fair basis to understand the amount.

Security Deposits Need a Careful Process

A security deposit is not a blank cheque for repairs. In Alberta, landlords need to follow the rules around condition inspections and deposit deductions. Move-in and move-out inspection reports are especially valuable because they create a written record of the unit’s condition before and after the tenancy.

Without good documentation, it becomes difficult to prove that a mark, broken fixture, or flooring issue was not already there. Photos dated at move-in and move-out can add useful context, particularly for paint, flooring, appliances, doors, and bathrooms.

At the end of a tenancy, landlords should provide a clear accounting of any proposed deductions and keep receipts, estimates, and repair records. Tenants should ask questions promptly if they disagree and keep copies of their own inspection reports, photos, emails, and repair requests.

A good process is not about making a deposit disappear. It is about identifying legitimate damage, charging fairly, and returning the remaining funds on time. If there is a serious disagreement, both sides may need formal tenancy-dispute guidance rather than trying to settle the matter through assumptions or text messages alone.

A Practical Way to Handle Damage During a Tenancy

The best time to deal with damage is when it happens, not at move-out. Tenants should notify the landlord as soon as possible, ideally in writing with a few clear photos. This is particularly important for leaks, water damage, broken locks, electrical issues, and anything that could worsen or affect safety.

Landlords should respond with a practical plan: confirm receipt, arrange an inspection or repair, explain whether the issue appears to be maintenance or tenant-caused damage, and provide an estimate if the tenant may be responsible. Avoid accusing a tenant before the facts are clear. A worn hinge and a broken hinge can look similar until someone checks the hardware and surrounding damage.

For small, straightforward jobs, it may make sense to agree on the repair before work begins. For larger damage, get a written estimate and document the existing condition. If the tenant accepts responsibility, confirm the amount and payment arrangement in writing. This keeps everyone clear on what is being repaired and why.

Landlords: Avoid These Common Mistakes

Disputes often start with poor documentation or delayed action, not bad intentions. Landlords are better protected when they avoid a few common shortcuts:

  • Charging for normal wear, aging fixtures, or routine maintenance.
  • Replacing an old item with a higher-end upgrade and billing the tenant the full cost.
  • Waiting until move-out to mention damage that was visible months earlier.
  • Making security deposit deductions without proper condition reports, photos, and itemized records.
  • Delaying urgent repairs while arguing about who will pay.

Tenants also have a role in preventing problems. They should use the home reasonably, report maintenance concerns early, avoid unapproved installations or alterations, and keep written records of repair requests. A tenant who reports a problem quickly is helping protect the property, even if the cause still needs to be assessed.

When Professional Repair Documentation Helps

A qualified handyman can be useful when the issue is minor enough that a larger contractor is not necessary but still needs to be repaired properly. Drywall patches, damaged doors, broken trim, loose flooring, failed caulking, faucet replacements, and wall repairs after removed mounts all benefit from clear workmanship and a straightforward invoice.

For landlords, a documented repair completed promptly helps return the unit to good condition and reduces vacancy delays. For tenants, it provides confidence that they are being charged for real work at a visible cost, not an inflated estimate.
