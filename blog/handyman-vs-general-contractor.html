Handyman vs General Contractor - Which to Hire?

By Vitalii Nestorenko · July 16, 2026 · 6 min read

A loose cabinet door, a damaged drywall corner, a leaky faucet, and a TV still sitting in its box can make a home feel like a never-ending to-do list. The handyman vs general contractor decision becomes much easier when you look at the scope of the work, not just the name of the person you are hiring. One is usually the practical choice for repairs and smaller upgrades. The other is built to manage major renovations with multiple moving parts.

Choosing the right fit can save you money, shorten the timeline, and prevent a small repair from becoming an overcomplicated project. It also helps you hire with confidence, especially when work needs to be completed quickly in an occupied home or rental property.

Handyman vs General Contractor: The Main Difference

A handyman handles a broad range of small-to-medium home repairs, maintenance tasks, installations, and light renovation work. The value is versatility. Instead of booking one person for drywall repair, another for a door adjustment, and a third for furniture assembly, you can often complete several practical jobs in one visit.

A general contractor is a project manager for larger construction or renovation work. They may perform some work directly, but their central role is coordinating the project: planning the schedule, arranging permits, hiring and supervising subcontractors, ordering materials, and keeping the renovation moving from demolition through final inspection.

The difference is not about one being better than the other. It is about the level of coordination your project requires. Replacing a toilet, repairing a fence panel, or installing a new light fixture is very different from rebuilding a kitchen, adding a bathroom, or removing structural walls.

When a Handyman Is the Right Call

A handyman is often the best choice when the work is clearly defined, relatively contained, and does not require a full construction crew. These jobs matter because they affect how your home functions every day, but they generally do not need architectural drawings, extensive permits, or a detailed trade schedule.

Common handyman projects include drywall patches, paint touch-ups, caulking, baseboard repairs, door installation or adjustment, flooring repairs, furniture assembly, TV mounting, blinds and shelving installation, deck and fence repairs, and debris removal. Many homeowners also book help for faucet or toilet replacement, light fixture replacement, and minor exterior maintenance.

This option makes particular sense when you have several unrelated tasks that have been waiting for attention. A reliable handyman can work through the list efficiently, helping you avoid the cost and scheduling hassle of calling several specialists for smaller jobs. For a landlord preparing a unit between tenants, that can mean faster turnover. For busy homeowners, it can mean getting the guest room, entryway, or backyard back in order before the weekend.

Speed is another advantage. Small repairs are often easier to schedule quickly, including same-day service when availability allows. You are paying for practical problem-solving and careful workmanship, not for a large project-management structure that the job does not need.

What a Handyman May Not Be the Best Fit For

There are limits, and they matter. A handyman should not be expected to take on work that requires specialized engineering, extensive permit coordination, major electrical or plumbing changes, gas work, structural alterations, or a multi-trade renovation. The exact requirements can depend on the project and local rules, so it is smart to ask early if permits or licensed trade work are involved.

A good professional will be direct about those boundaries. If a repair reveals a larger issue behind a wall, under flooring, or around plumbing, the right next step may be a specialized trade or a general contractor. Clear advice at that point protects your home and your budget.

When You Need a General Contractor

Hire a general contractor when your project has multiple stages, multiple trades, and a meaningful risk of delays or expensive mistakes without close coordination. Kitchen renovations, full bathroom remodels, basement developments, additions, major layout changes, and structural work are typical examples.

For instance, a bathroom renovation can involve demolition, plumbing, electrical, ventilation, waterproofing, tile, cabinetry, drywall, painting, and inspections. Even if each trade is skilled, someone needs to arrange the order. The plumber may need access before walls are closed. Tile cannot begin until waterproofing is complete. Fixtures need to arrive before final installation. A general contractor manages those dependencies and takes responsibility for the larger picture.

They are also the better choice when permits, drawings, inspections, or building-code questions are central to the work. In Calgary, renovation requirements can vary based on what is changing, particularly where structural, electrical, plumbing, or secondary-suite work is involved. A contractor experienced with projects of that size can help establish the proper process before work begins.

This service comes with a higher cost because it includes more planning, administration, supervision, and often a network of subcontractors. That added layer is worthwhile when it prevents the project from stalling or puts one accountable person in charge of a complex renovation.

Compare Cost by Scope, Not Hourly Rate

It is tempting to compare only the hourly rate or the first quote you receive. That can lead to the wrong hire. A general contractor may appear expensive for a simple repair because their business is designed to manage larger projects. A handyman may look like the lower-cost option, but hiring one for work outside their proper scope can create costly delays or require the job to be redone.

For small tasks, a handyman often provides better value because the work can be completed in a focused visit. If you need a curtain rod installed, a door that sticks fixed, a drywall hole patched, and a few shelves mounted, grouping those jobs is usually more efficient than treating each one as a separate service call.

For a renovation, ask the contractor what is included in the quote. Clarify allowances for materials, permit fees, demolition, disposal, subcontractors, site protection, and changes discovered after work starts. A lower initial number is not automatically the better number if key costs have been left out.

Questions to Ask Before You Book

The right questions make hiring less stressful. Start by describing the outcome you want, then ask whether the professional regularly handles that kind of work. You should also confirm whether permits or specialized trades may be required, how pricing is structured, what materials you need to provide, and how long the work is expected to take.

For either type of provider, ask about insurance, experience with similar homes, cleanup, and how they handle unexpected issues. You want someone who protects your space, communicates before making changes, and leaves the work area tidy. For a larger project, also ask who will be on site each day and who is responsible for scheduling trades and inspections.

Photos are helpful before a booking, but they do not always tell the whole story. Water damage, hidden framing issues, old wiring, and uneven surfaces can change the scope once work begins. A professional should explain that possibility without using it as an excuse for vague pricing.

A Simple Way to Make the Decision

Choose a handyman if you can describe the project as a repair, installation, maintenance task, or light upgrade that can be completed without coordinating a full renovation. Choose a general contractor if your project changes the layout, involves several trades, requires permits and inspections, or needs someone to manage a longer schedule.

There is a middle ground, too. You may hire a handyman for the finishing work around a renovation, such as mounting accessories, adjusting doors, completing touch-up paint, repairing trim, or assembling furniture after the larger construction is complete. That is often a practical way to keep a contractor focused on the major build while still getting the final details handled properly.
